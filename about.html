About this project

How we tested, and who we are

Appliance Energy Australia is a student project that turns public appliance data into clear, honest visualisations. This page is the method and the fine print behind every number on the site.

Why this site exists

Energy labels contain useful information, but the numbers are easy to overlook in a shop. This site aims to show that information in a way people can explore, so they can compare appliances and understand what their choices mean for their power bill.

Who it is for

The Televisions page is written for Australian households choosing a new TV. They want to know whether a bigger screen, or a low star rating, will noticeably increase their power bill, and how to compare models in the shop.

How we tested

Source

The site uses appliance registration data published by the Australian Government through the Energy Rating program. The television data set is the extract dated 15 February 2026. Each row is one registered model, with its screen size, screen technology, star rating and labelled energy consumption.

Processing

The data was cleaned and summarised in KNIME. Only models marked as available and sold in Australia were kept, and brand names that appeared in more than one form were merged. Screen size was converted from centimetres to inches and grouped into small (43 inches or less), medium (44 to 65) and large (66 or more). Average energy use and an example yearly cost were then calculated for each size group and star rating, and exported as CSV files for the charts.

Privacy

The data set describes products, not people. It contains no personal information, only brand and model details submitted by the companies that register their televisions.

Accuracy and limitations

Energy figures come from a standard test that assumes 10 hours of viewing a day, so real use will differ. Standby power is not included in the running costs. A few screen sizes look like they may have been entered incorrectly. The data counts models, not sales, so a brand with many models is not necessarily the most popular. The $0.33/kWh price is an example, and real tariffs vary by state and retailer.

Ethics

The site does not recommend any brand or model. Charts start at zero and use the same scale within each chart so differences are not exaggerated. Averages are shown with the number of models behind them where the group is small.

How it is built

The pages use HTML, a single external CSS file and plain JavaScript. The findings charts were made in KNIME and exported as images; the calculator's comparison chart is drawn live with the D3.js library. The code is kept in a public GitHub repository, and the site is built to be hosted on Swinburne's Mercury web server.

Modifications and reflection

What I changed or adapted after generation: After generating the website, I ran all the KNIME workflows and exported the charts for the website. I changed star rating, shown as line plot and limited the brand chart to the top 10 brands to make the results easier to understand. I also checked the numbers shown on the website against my KNIME output to make sure they were consistent. During this process, I dealt with some issues involving duplicate brand names, CSV exports, and chart labels.

What I learned: I learned how to use KNIME to clean, filter, and group data before creating visualisations. I also learned that the way data is filtered and grouped can affect the results and the conclusions drawn from a chart. Another thing I learned was the importance of checking chart labels and exported data carefully because small mistakes can make the information misleading. Overall, this project helped me understand how data processing and visualisation work together to communicate findings clearly.

What helped and what misled me: KNIME helped me organise the television data and generate the results needed for the website. However, duplicate brand names made the brand analysis more difficult, and CSV export settings sometimes affected how the data was read. I also found that chart labels needed to be checked carefully to ensure that the titles and units matched the actual data being displayed. These issues taught me not to rely on generated outputs without checking them first.

Limitations: The electricity price of $0.33/kWh used in the calculator is only an example, so actual running costs may be different depending on the user’s electricity tariff. The dataset contains registered television models rather than actual sales figures, so the number of models cannot be used to determine which brands are the most popular. Actual electricity costs also depend on viewing habits, including how many hours the television is used each day. Therefore, the results should be treated as estimates for comparison rather than exact predictions of household electricity bills.
